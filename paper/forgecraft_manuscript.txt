When does a manufacturability term change what evolution selects? 
Measurement, repair and a computable screening bound, with the 
ForgeCraft platform 
Zhenxin Liu¹*, Open Researcher and Contributor Identifier (ORCID) 0009-0005-0773-647X 
¹ Beijing Jiaotong University, Beijing, China 
* Corresponding author. E-mail: liuzhenxin0611@qq.com 
Abstract 
Background. Morphology–control co-design searches simulated link-and-joint assemblies, so the part 
geometry, tolerances and clearances that decide whether a design can be built never enter the objective. 
Adding a manufacturability term is the usual remedy, and its effectiveness is usually assumed rather than 
measured. Aim. We ask when such a term can change what selection keeps, and we answer it with 
measurement rather than with a new search algorithm. The vehicle is ForgeCraft, an open-source Python 
platform that couples declarative part catalogues, graph neural network (GNN) co-evolution with 
proximal policy optimisation (PPO) controllers, MuJoCo evaluation, and an export layer emitting 
tessellated assemblies in the Standard for the Exchange of Product model data (STEP), application 
protocol 242 (AP242), drawings in Drawing Exchange Format (DXF), interference reports and bills of 
materials. Method. We ablate the term counterfactually within single runs, on two instrument builds, and 
derive a bound on the reordering any weight can produce. Results. The shipped build (tag v0.4.0) could 
not act on the term at all: the score's range lay inside [0.80, 1.00] and its weight was never read from the 
task file, so every run silently used 0.05. After repair the score's mean within-catalogue range rises from 
0.024 to 0.225 and the term does reorder individuals, but only a minority of comparisons: at the deployed 
weight the discordant count has a median of 1 of 120 pairs on one catalogue and 3 of 120 on the other, 
non-zero in 7 and 10 of 13 seeds, and top-five membership changes in 1 and 6 of 13 seeds. A pair can 
reverse only when its fitness gap is smaller than the term's contribution. The fraction of such pairs is 
computable in one generation and bounds reordering from above; its median is 12–29% for one catalogue 
and 16–68% for the other over a fortyfold weight sweep, which is why the same nominal weight behaves 
differently in each. We condense the bound into a five-case screening protocol that decides, from one 
generation and without an evolutionary run, whether a given weight is worth a seed budget. Whether 
crossing that bound improves populations is still open: at thirteen seeds per arm the mean difference is 
indistinguishable from zero, and the power calculation asks for 6 seeds at a difference of 0.10 and 57 at 
0.03. Limitation. No evolved design has been fabricated, and the export layer reveals defects the 
objective never sees: every final individual contains colliding part pairs. 
Keywords: evolutionary robotics · morphology optimisation · design for additive manufacturing · STEP 
AP242 · reproducibility · open-source software 
1 Introduction 
The body of a robot largely determines what that robot can do, and in practice the body is normally fixed by 
human designers before any controller is learned. Co-design — the joint optimisation of morphology and control 
— has been studied since the first work on evolving virtual creatures [1] and has been revisited repeatedly with 
modern machinery: graph-grammar design spaces [2], learned transform-and-control policies [4], self-assembling 
modular morphologies [5], and quality-diversity search over collections of designs [6, 7]. 
A persistent limitation runs through this literature. Evaluated designs are almost always simulated bodies: abstract 
link-and-joint assemblies whose geometric realisation is left to the reader. An evolved morphology cannot be built, 
however, without answering questions the simulation never asks. Which abstract links correspond to printable 
parts? Do they fit a build volume? Are the walls thick enough? Do adjacent parts collide once real bearings, 
fasteners and clearances are introduced? Can the joint be assembled at all, and what does the bill of materials cost? 
These are not cosmetic concerns: they decide whether an evolved design leaves the computer. When 
manufacturability is absent from the objective, search is free to exploit shapes that are optimal in simulation and 
unbuildable in reality, and a post-hoc buildability filter then discards exactly the designs the search converged on. 
Two further gaps compound the problem. Reproducibility: morphology-evolution results are frequently reported 
for bespoke codebases that cannot be re-run, so comparisons between methods remain approximate. 

Fragmentation: the capabilities required for a complete study — morphology generation, physics evaluation, 
control learning, geometric modelling and manufacturing preparation — are normally distributed across separate 
tools with incompatible data models, so every stage boundary is crossed by hand. 
Contribution. The measurement is the contribution; the platform is what made it possible. (i) A counterfactual 
method that removes an objective term within a single run, so that no re-evaluation is needed and unpaired worker 
seeds cannot contaminate the comparison. (ii) A two-build measurement showing that the shipped release could 
not act on its manufacturing term at all, and identifying the two instrumentation defects responsible — a threshold-
triggered score with no gradient, and a weight lookup that read the wrong configuration namespace, so every task 
silently used a hard-coded fallback. (iii) A bound, proved in Section 7.5 and verified on every cell of two sweeps, 
on how many pairs of individuals any weight can reorder, together with the single-generation quantity that makes 
the bound usable before a long run is spent: the fraction of pairs whose fitness gap is smaller than the term's 
contribution. (iv) A replication budget — 6 to 57 seeds per arm, for differences of 0.10 down to 0.03 — that states 
what evidence a population-level claim would require, and a five-case screening protocol (Section 7.10) that 
decides from one generation whether a given weight is worth spending that budget, with each case instantiated by 
a row of our own sweep. The platform itself contributes an explicit parametric representation of evolved bodies as 
mechanical parts sharing parameters with the bodies that were evaluated, and an export chain that converts such a 
body into the deliverables a fabricator can consume. We also report, in Section 8, the defects and inconsistencies 
found in our own release, because a framework making manufacturing claims is only as useful as its account of 
where those claims currently stop. 
2 Related work and the position of this work 
Modern co-design systems encode a body as a graph and optimise it with evolutionary search while a learned 
controller is fitted to each candidate; ForgeCraft follows that template and differs in the treatment of the output. 
The body graph is not only a simulation input but also the specification from which concrete mechanical geometry 
is generated, so the artefact evaluated and the artefact exported derive from one shared parameter set (Section 3.4). 
Three platforms are close enough in ambition to warrant direct comparison, and Table 1 summarises it. Evolution 
Gym [8] is a large-scale benchmark for co-optimising the design and control of soft robots, in which a body is a 
2D lattice of material cells simulated as a mass-spring system. ARIEL (https://ci-group.github.io/ariel/), the 
successor to the Revolve simulator [9], covers genome encoding, evolution, MuJoCo simulation and result 
analysis for modular robots. JBotEvolver [10] is a long-established Java platform for evolutionary robotics 
research and teaching, built around a 2D differential-drive engine. 
All three are strong at what they set out to do, and ForgeCraft integrates the same class of components rather than 
reimplementing them. The relevant difference is the terminal stage: in the released feature sets of all three, the 
pipeline ends once a simulation result has been produced and logged, and none describes a route from an evolved 
body to manufacturing deliverables. For Evolution Gym this follows from the representation itself — a 2D voxel 
matrix has no corresponding fabrication output. For ARIEL and JBotEvolver, whose robots are built from physical 
modules or wheeled bases, the morphologies are realisable in hardware by construction, but the mapping from an 
evolved graph to machine-readable part geometry, drawings and bills of materials is not part of the framework. 
Design for additive manufacturing [11] and design for manufacture and assembly [12] supply the criteria a 
buildable part must satisfy — minimum feature size, wall thickness, build-volume fit, support and orientation, 
assembly complexity — and product data exchange between computer-aided design (CAD) systems is 
standardised in International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 10303, whose AP242 part [13] covers 
managed model-based 3D engineering, with general tolerances given by ISO 2768-1 [14]. This body of work is 
mature, but it does not address a design generated by a search free to exploit any unmodelled degree of freedom — 
precisely the case ForgeCraft is built for.

3 System overview 
ForgeCraft is a layered Python package of 192 modules (approximately 60,000 lines) with a deliberately narrow 
interface between layers, so that individual research questions can be addressed without modifying the rest of the 
stack. 
3.1 Core morphology representation 
The central data structure is MechanicalBody, a directed graph of Part and Joint objects. Parts carry a type and a 
parameter dictionary, so a body is a typed graph rather than a fixed topology; the representation is memory-
conscious by design (__slots__ on both classes) because populations of thousands of bodies are held in memory 
during search. Parts are drawn from a catalogue: a declarative YAML description of the available part types with 
their geometric primitive, parameter ranges, mass, friction and actuation properties. Because the catalogue is data 
rather than code, a new design space is a configuration file rather than a fork; 21 catalogues ship with the release, 
spanning primitive parts, legged robots, grippers, hoppers and parameterised part libraries. 
3.2 Physics evaluation 
Evolved bodies are compiled to MuJoCo models in the MuJoCo Extensible Markup Language (XML) format 
(MJCF) for rigid-body simulation, with an optional Genesis backend for GPU-accelerated evaluation. To keep 
search tractable, the evaluation path uses deliberately simplified collision geometry (convex proxies), which 
decouples the cost of physics evaluation from the cost of geometric detail. Environments follow the Gymnasium 

API [15], and task specifications — reward composition, episode budget, termination criteria — are themselves 
declarative YAML. 
One modelling detail determines whether the reward terms carry any signal at all: the MJCF builder attaches a 
<freejoint/> to the root body, so every compiled model has a full six-degree-of-freedom floating base. An 
earlier revision omitted that element and welded the root to the world frame, which made the displacement-, 
speed- and fall-based reward terms identically zero. Section 6 records the measured before-and-after effect of the 
repair. 
3.3 Search 
Co-evolution alternates between population-level search over morphologies and reinforcement-learning updates of 
the controllers conditioned on those morphologies. Morphologies are encoded by a message-passing graph neural 
network [16, 17] into fixed-length embeddings, and controllers are PPO [18] actors conditioned on those 
embeddings. Conditioning the policy on a morphology embedding allows a controller to be trained for an arbitrary 
topology, but ForgeCraft trains a separate controller per evaluated individual within that individual's evaluation 
budget, optionally warm-started from the previous generation: it does not learn a single policy that generalises 
zero-shot across body topologies, and we make no such claim. Selection combines elitism, tournament selection 
and Pareto-based multi-objective selection through the non-dominated sorting genetic algorithms (NSGA) of [19, 
20], while a multi-dimensional archive of phenotypic elites [6] maintains behavioural diversity. Search is made 
affordable by adaptive evaluation budgets that grow with the generation index, and runs support checkpoint and 
resume. 
3.4 Geometry and manufacturing layer 
A parametric generator produces concrete mechanical geometry for each abstract part — chassis with ribs and 
counterbores, motors with cooling fins and end bells, bearings with shields, tubes with threaded ends, springs, feet 
and battery packs — from the same parameter dictionary used during search. Nine classes of part are generated at 
four vertex-density presets, and parts receive physically based rendering materials from ten presets. Because 
search-time bodies and export-time geometry share parameters, the exported artefacts correspond to the design that 
was actually evaluated. Optional modules provide finite-element and isogeometric analysis, additive-
manufacturing pre-processing, knowledge storage and LLM-assisted design agents, isolated behind optional 
installation extras so that the core pipeline keeps a small dependency footprint; Section 8 records that these 
analysis modules are incomplete and unused in every result reported here. 
4 The manufacturing-aware export chain 
The export chain is the contribution claimed in this paper. A single entry point, export_all_enhanced, takes an 
evolved body plus the catalogue it was drawn from and performs the following stages end to end: 
1. Parametric solid generation for every part in the evolved body. 
2. Connection features — bolt holes, hinge bearing seats and snap fits generated from the joint type, with 
clearances derived from fused deposition modelling (FDM) tolerance profiles. 
3. Tolerance and shrinkage compensation for the selected print profile. 
4. Support and orientation planning, with support-volume reporting. 
5. Interference checking between mating part pairs, classifying each fit as clearance, transition or interference. 
6. STEP AP242 assembly export for CAD interchange, subject to the qualification below. 
7. 2D engineering drawings in DXF with tolerance annotation. 
8. Bill of materials with mass roll-up and material recommendations. 
9. Manufacturability scoring, decomposed into build-volume, wall-thickness, assembly-complexity, torque-
budget and cantilever checks. 
10. Production packaging into one archive that can be handed to a fabricator, together with a Unified Robot 
Description Format (URDF) file for downstream robotics tooling and an optional export of the trained policy 
to the Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX) format. 
Two properties matter more than the stage list. First, the manufacturability score described in stage 9 is wired into 
the search layer — a manufacturability term and a minimum-score constraint are both available per task — and 
Section 7 measures what that wiring currently achieves. Second, the STEP output carries a qualification we will 
not bury: the exported entities are tessellated (TESSELLATED_ITEM with 
COORDINATES_LIST and 
TRIANGULATED_FACE, under the AP242_MANAGED_MODEL_BASED_3D_ENGINEERING_MIM_LF schema). The file is 
valid AP242 and readable by tessellation-aware viewers, but it carries triangle meshes rather than analytic non-
uniform rational B-spline (NURBS) surfaces and topological B-rep edges, so it cannot be edited parametrically on 

import. A fabricator receiving this package would typically rebuild parametric features from the DXF drawings 
and the bill of materials rather than from the solid. Closing that gap is future work. 
5 Worked example: one complete run 
The workflow is exercised by an example committed to the repository (examples/demo_robot/), recording the 
artefacts of a finished evolution run (generation 89, individual 005), started with a single command: 
python -m forgecraft.main --catalog speedster --task speed --population 32 --
generations 100 --seed 42 --export --export-dir design_output 
The command-line interface (CLI) --export flag drives the lightweight export path (stereolithography (STL) and 
STEP meshes, 3MF, URDF, a bill of materials (BOM) and a review report). The fuller presentation chain, which 
additionally produces DXF drawings, glTF scenes, rendered views and the packed production archive, is 
export_all_enhanced and is reached through the Python API. 
The recorded design comprises 25 parts and 24 joints with a stored composite fitness of 12954.87, dominated by 
movement terms (displacement = 10452.36, speed = 2497.80, upright = 46.41). The body has eight actuated 
joints, so the review report no longer emits the "no actuated joints, the body cannot move under its own power" 
warning that the previous snapshot carried. We nevertheless report the manufacturing diagnostics in full, because 
they say more about the state of the release than the fitness value does (Table 2, Fig. 1).

Three caveats follow, and we state them rather than omit them. First, a stored fitness of 12954.87 is not evidence 
of a high-performing robot: it is a weighted sum dominated by movement terms, reached while the body's parts 
interpenetrate in 120 pairs and its joints admit physically meaningless travel, neither of which the objective 
penalises. The honest reading is that the objective now carries a locomotion signal — which, before the repair 
described in Section 6, it did not — and that self-collision and joint-limit terms must be added before evolved 
bodies can be read as engineering designs. Second, the aggregate figures disagree: overall (0.9925) and 
manufacturability_score (0.70) are two distinct definitions rather than an arithmetic error (Section 7), while 
the mass of this single body is reported as 5.244 kg, 6.233 kg and 7.005 kg by three sites, and cost as 157.31 
against 442.11 in United States (US) dollars (USD). Each site derives volume and unit cost from a different 
formula — bounding-box extents, per-part parameters, or a separate price table — and we report all of these 
figures rather than the most flattering one. Third, the committed snapshot is a stored artefact: STL, STEP, DXF, 
URDF and the packed archive regenerate from best_body.json and are deliberately not committed, to keep the 
repository small. 
We therefore present this example as evidence for three narrow claims only: that the pipeline runs end to end from 
a task specification to a production package, that evolutionary search now receives a locomotion signal, and that 
the export chain emits inspectable, machine-readable deliverables. It is not evidence that the framework 
discovered a high-performing robot. 
6 Verification and reproducibility 
The repository contains 424 automated tests — 253 in the core suite and 171 module-level — covering 
morphology graph invariants, catalogue loading, genetic operators, selection schemes, checkpoint round-trip, 
physics model construction, geometry generation, tolerance and connector calculation, interference checking and 
export formats. Continuous integration runs the suite on Python 3.10–3.12 at every commit, skipping GPU-
dependent tests when no Compute Unified Device Architecture (CUDA) device is present. Runs are seeded, task 
and catalogue definitions are declarative, and checkpoints capture both evolutionary and training state, so an 
interrupted run resumes to an identical configuration. 
The most consequential defect found while preparing this release illustrates why a green test suite is not sufficient 
evidence. In the previous revision, forgecraft/simulation/builder.py emitted the root body without a 
<freejoint/>, so a six-part, five-joint body compiled with no floating base: the centre-of-mass sensor was 
constant, termination criteria of the form _com_z < fall_height never fired, and the displacement, speed and 
velocity reward terms were identically zero, removing the locomotion signal from the objective altogether. The 
repair is verified by measurement rather than assertion: the same body now compiles to nq = 9, nv = 8, with the 
free joint at qposadr = 0 and hinges at qposadr = 7 and 8; in a 300-step zero-action rollout the centre of mass 
descends from 0.476 m to 0.191 m, translates 0.064 m along x and fires the fall termination at step 299 — all 
exactly zero before the repair. Two latent Gymnasium-contract errors in forgecraft/rl/env.py were exposed by 
the same investigation and are fixed with it: step() returned np.bool_ rather than bool, and reset() perturbed 
qpos by joint index, which with a floating base corrupts the base position and quaternion. 
7 What a manufacturing term actually does to selection 
The framework's design goal is that buildability is optimised rather than filtered. We tested that claim on two 
instrument builds of the same codebase. The shipped build (tag v0.4.0) returned a clean negative; investigating 
why exposed two instrumentation defects, which we report and repair. The repaired build (commit a246c14) 
returns a different answer: the term can reorder a population only to the extent that individuals are separated by 
fitness gaps smaller than its own contribution, and that extent is measurable in one generation. What the repaired 
build does not return is a population-level improvement, and Section 7.7 states the replication that would be 
needed to look for one. 
7.1 Removing the term without re-running 
Comparing two independently evolved arms is unreliable in this codebase: evaluation runs in worker processes, 
and on Windows those workers are spawned rather than forked, so they do not inherit the parent seed and two 
same-seed arms are not strictly paired. We therefore measured within a single run. Each individual carries a fitness 
that includes the manufacturability term and a directly measurable value of that term, so the counterfactual fitness 
obtained by removing it is fitness_without = fitness_with − w_mfg · mfg, with no re-running required. 

The effective weight is w_mfg = 0.05: the task file lists manufacturability among its fitness components but 
declares no matching reward component, so get_weight returns zero and the aggregator's fallback applies (defect 
D2 in Section 7.3). Every statistic in this section is recomputed from the stored final populations by 
recompute_tables.py and aggregated across seeds by aggregate_seeds.py in the ablation experiment directory, 
which read the same JSON files the runs wrote. 
7.2 First measurement (v0.4.0): the term never acted 
On the shipped build the term does not change selection. Across 20 generations and a population of 16, 
removing the term reversed the ordering of no pair of individuals in either catalogue (Table 3). One member of the 
top five changes identity in parameterized_parts_v7, at a boundary that falls inside a three-way fitness tie — 
the kind of change that cannot alter which individuals selection retains. Repeating the measurement on the repaired 
build over thirteen seeds changes the conclusion in degree but not in kind: the term is weak rather than absent. The 
discordant count has a median of 1 of 120 pairs on primitives and 3 on parameterized_parts_v7, it is non-zero 
in 7 and 10 of the 13 seeds, and top-five membership changes in 1 and 6 of them. A term that reorders a handful of 
comparisons in a majority of runs can be measured but cannot be expected to steer a population, which is the 
question Section 7.7 takes up.

that answers the question actually being asked, and it is zero on both shipped rows. We report this because the tie 
structure is itself an independent property of the release: a population in which a third of individuals share the 
score 0.0 has limited resolution for any added objective term, however well designed. 
7.3 Two instrumentation defects 
A null result invites the question of whether the instrument was working. It was not, in two independent ways. 
D1 — the scoring function had no gradient. The five checks (build volume, wall thickness, assembly 
complexity, torque budget, cantilever) were each written as a threshold test with a capped penalty. A part either 
passed, losing nothing, or failed, losing a fixed amount, so the theoretical range of the composite score was [0.80, 
1.00] and in practice almost nothing triggered. Sampling 100 randomly generated bodies from each of the 21 
shipped catalogues, the score was identically constant for 7 of the 21 — no body differed from any other body. A 
further defect sat inside the torque check: when a body had no actuated joints the check returned 1.0, so a 
morphology that cannot move under its own power received full marks for its torque budget. Because no 
combination of checks could move the composite by more than 0.20, no two individuals could ever be separated 
by a manufacturability contribution larger than 0.05 × 0.20 = 0.01, which is why the v0.4.0 rows of Table 3 report 
spreads of 0.019 and 0.069 and no reordering. 
D2 — the weight was never connected. aggregate_fitness_generic obtained the manufacturability weight 
through get_weight, which reads reward.components — the namespace that configures the reinforcement-
learning reward. The task files declared manufacturability in fitness_components and fitness_formula 
instead, and fitness_formula was used only to compose a description string. The lookup therefore always 
missed, and the aggregator silently fell back to a hard-coded 0.05. The weights written in the task files — 0.03 for 
multi_objective, 0.01 for speed_density — never took effect. This is the more consequential of the two 

defects, because it is invisible: a declared weight that is silently replaced by a default produces a run that looks 
correct and is not. 
7.4 Repair 
Both defects are fixed in the release accompanying this paper. 
• 
All five checks are now graded rather than binary: a design threshold maps to a score of 0.5, with headroom 
above it and progressive loss below it. The torque check reports not applicable for bodies without hinge joints 
and is dropped from the weighted sum, which is then renormalised over the applicable checks; a body with 
actuated joints and zero available torque now scores 0.0 rather than 1.0. 
• 
A separate fitness_weights namespace is introduced, distinct from the reward configuration. The 
aggregator resolves weights in the order explicit fitness weight, then reward component, then fallback — and 
the fallback emits a warning instead of passing silently. 
• 
The internal weights are redistributed according to the resolving power of each check: assembly complexity 
0.35, torque budget 0.25, cantilever 0.15, wall thickness 0.15, build volume 0.10.

7.5 A bound on reordering, and the statistic that makes it usable 
The bound. Write f for fitness including the term, m for the score, and g = f − w·m for the counterfactual. A pair 
(i, j) reverses only if sign(f_i − f_j) ≠ sign(g_i − g_j); since g_i − g_j = (f_i − f_j) − w(m_i − 
m_j), that requires |f_i − f_j| < w·|m_i − m_j| ≤ w·Δmfg. Reversals are therefore a subset of the pairs 
whose fitness gap is smaller than the term's maximum contribution. The size of that subset, as a fraction of all 
pairs, is an upper bound on the discordant fraction. The bound is exact rather than asymptotic, it requires one 
generation and no counterfactual run, and it is the quantity a practitioner can compute before committing to a 
weight. 
The single-generation statistic. We call that fraction the at-risk fraction A(w). It is tighter than the range-based 
form, because |m_i − m_j| is replaced by the actual pairwise difference rather than its maximum: at w = 0.05 on 
primitives, a median of 22 of 120 pairs fall below w·Δmfg but only 15 fall below w·|m_i − m_j|. The ratio r = 
w·Δmfg/σ(fitness) orders the two catalogues correctly and is the cheapest summary of the same information, but 
it is a proxy; the sweep below shows why it should not be read as a threshold.

superseded claim is precisely the one a reader would carry away. 
Where the bound applies. The inequality holds for any scalarised objective and any weight, so it needs no 
assumption about the metric's design. Its usefulness is narrower: it is informative only while A(w) is small, it 
evaluates a single generation of the population that selection will actually act on, and it says nothing about steady-
state or archive-based selection, where the surviving set is not produced by one ranking. It also cannot say whether 
a reversal is beneficial — only whether it is possible. It is a screening device for deciding whether a weight is 
worth running, not a design rule that a weight can be read off. 
7.6 Why a weighted sum rather than a third objective 
Why the release scalarises. The aggregator composes a scalar fitness, and every search operator in the release 
consumes that scalar: the generational loop, the NSGA front construction and the phenotypic-elites archive all 
rank individuals by it. Adding manufacturability as a weighted component is the only change that leaves those 
operators untouched, which is why the release was built that way. The choice is nevertheless not neutral for the 
result, and the stored populations say by how much. 
Quantifying the alternative. Treating fitness and score as two objectives — the minimal form of the third-
objective proposal — leaves 3 of 16 individuals non-dominated on the primitives population, and the most 
manufacturable individual (mfg = 1.000) ranks eighth of sixteen in fitness. A weighted sum at w = 0.05 cannot 
retain that individual; a Pareto rule retains it by construction. On parameterized_parts_v7 the front collapses to 
a single individual, because the fittest individual happens also to be the most manufacturable. The two catalogues 
therefore differ in kind and not only in degree: the one in which the weighted sum fails most visibly is also the one 
in which a Pareto rule would act most strongly. 
What this means for the result. Sections 7.2 and 7.7 measure the behaviour of a weighted sum. They do not 
measure the informativeness of the score, and the distinction matters: under a Pareto rule the term would act on the 
surviving set with no weight at all, and the replication problem of Section 7.7 would disappear along with the 

weight. We did not run that comparison and we do not claim it would favour the term — on a sixteen-individual 
population a two-objective front of three members is itself a small and unstable object. The experiment that would 
settle it is specific: the same two catalogues and thirteen seeds, nsga2 with objectives (task fitness, 
manufacturability, behavioural diversity), reporting the distribution of manufacturability along the final front and 
the same discordance statistics. Until it is run, the negative result of this section is properly read as a statement 
about scalarisation. 
7.7 Does crossing the bound improve the population? 
The bound concerns a single generation. A separate claim would be that choosing a weight large enough to reorder 
individuals causes evolution to produce more manufacturable populations over many generations. We tested that 
claim directly, because it does not follow from the bound and it is the claim a reader would actually want. 
Using parameterized_parts_v7 and the speed task, we ran two arms, w = 0.05 (baseline) and w = 0.50 
(treatment, a weight that clears the bound on this catalogue), for 20 generations at population 16, over 13 seeds.

deviation measured on the full sample is smaller, so the same calculation now asks for 6, and the experiment is 
comfortably sized for the largest difference considered here. Each run takes about five minutes and runs are serial 
because concurrent evaluation exceeds available memory, which is why the budget is quoted in hours. Because the 
evaluation workers do not inherit the parent seed (Section 7.1), same-seed arms are not strictly paired, so we report 
the per-seed differences descriptively and test the two arms as independent samples. The measured quantity is the 
mean manufacturability of the final population (Table 6).

shift of a few hundredths in a mean. 
The same calculation then states what the remaining question costs. With the standard deviation measured on the 
full sample (0.057), resolving an effect of 0.10 would need 6 seeds per arm, 0.07 would need 11, 0.05 would need 
21 and 0.03 would need 57. The honest conclusion is therefore not that raising the weight fails, but that deciding 
whether it succeeds costs between one and several days of computation per question at the current evaluation cost 
— and that this estimate is itself optimistic while same-seed arms remain unpaired. 
Reducing variance is more economical than adding seeds, and the ordering of remedies is clear from where the 
variance comes from: make evaluation reproducible and genuinely paired so that a seed identifies a run rather than 

merely a starting point; run long enough that final populations have converged, so that trajectory stochasticity 
stops contributing; and only then enlarge the population and add seeds. 
7.8 Two different quantities share one name 
compute_manufacturability applies the five geometric checks described above and is what the search layer 
consumes. score_manufacturability instead scores structure, materials, tolerances and cost, with the structure 
term driven by the number of colliding part pairs, and is what the exported report quotes. This is the origin of an 
inconsistency recorded in the repository documentation, where the reference body reports overall 0.9925 beside 
manufacturability_score 0.70 in the same file: the two numbers answer different questions. The export-side 
score is itself close to constant, returning exactly 0.70 for 20 of the 21 catalogues. 
7.9 What does work: the export layer 
The defects the objective cannot see are detected downstream, and they are the norm rather than the exception. In 
every run reported here, all 16 individuals in the final population contain colliding part pairs, with up to 151 
colliding pairs in a single body, while the fitness-side score of those same bodies remains above 0.97. The 
reference body of Section 5 carries 120 such pairs and 11 out-of-range joint limits. Self-collision is therefore not a 
tail case that a filter would catch at the end; it is the typical state of the population, and the interference report is 
the only component of the framework that surfaces it. 
7.10 A screening protocol and its consequence for the contribution 
The preceding subsections amount to a decision procedure that costs one evaluated generation and needs no 
evolutionary run. We state it as a protocol because a negative result is only useful if it is portable: a reader with a 
different metric, a different population and a different weight should be able to decide in one generation whether 
the term can matter, instead of spending seeds until the budget is gone. 
From the final population of an ordinary run at the deployed weight w, compute three quantities. First, the at-risk 
fraction A(w) of Section 7.5; by the bound, no reversal can occur outside that set, so A(w) is a ceiling on what the 
term can do at that weight. Second, the discordant count D(w), the reversals that do occur in g = f − w·m; this is 
what the term does to this population. Third, the number of individuals entering or leaving the top-k set of the 
current ranking, at the k = 5 and k = 10 granularity of Table 5; this is the only one of the three that changes 
which individuals the next generation is built from. Listing 1 computes all three from the two vectors a run already 
stores, in O(n²) and without an evaluator call. 
def screen(f, m, w, k): 
    pairs = [(i, j) for i in range(len(f)) for j in range(i + 1, len(f))] 
    at_risk = sum(1 for i, j in pairs if abs(f[i] - f[j]) < w * abs(m[i] - m[j])) 
    g = [fi - w * mi for fi, mi in zip(f, m)] 
    disc = sum(1 for i, j in pairs if (f[i] - f[j]) * (g[i] - g[j]) < 0) 
    top = lambda v: set(sorted(range(len(v)), key=lambda i: -v[i])[:k]) 
    return at_risk / len(pairs), disc, len(top(f) ^ top(g)) 
The three quantities separate five cases, and only the last of them justifies a seed budget (Table 7). Each case is 
instantiated by a row of Table 5, so the protocol is not a hypothetical construction: it re-derives from a single 
generation what the sweep establishes by enumeration.

Applied to our own runs, the protocol would have stopped the reported experiment twice. On primitives at w =

further seeds could have created an effect that the comparisons do not contain. On parameterized_parts_v7 at 

the same weight the median is 19 at-risk pairs and 3 discordant ones, and the top five does change in 6 of the 13 
seeds — the term acts there, but in a minority of runs and largely where the population is close to tied; in the seed-

places (0.245403) while their scores differ by 0.019. On primitives at w = 0.2 a reversal occurs in 9 of the 13 
seeds while the top five stays put in 12 of them, which is case 4. The protocol first passes at w = 0.5 on 
parameterized_parts_v7 (median A = 35/120, D = 12, top-five changed in 6 of 13 seeds), which is exactly the 
arm Section 7.7 ran; the outcome of that run is that the per-generation signal is real and the population-level effect 
is still below what thirteen seeds can resolve. 
The classification depends on k, and the dependence is informative rather than a nuisance. At k = 3 the 
primitives population at w = 0.05 moves from case 2 to case 3, because the two individuals tied at 2.476959 
straddle the third rank: the term then changes the elite set without reversing a single comparison. A term whose 
only action is to choose between bodies the evaluator cannot separate is worth having as a tie-breaker, but it is not 
worth a seed budget, and the protocol says so at whichever k the reader applies it. 
The claim this section supports is narrow and, we think, more useful than the one we started with. It is not that 
manufacturability can be optimised by adding a weight, and not that it cannot; it is that the question is decidable 
before a run is spent, and that the answer depends jointly on the weight, the metric's spread and the population's 
gap structure. Three consequences follow. First, a term can be present in the objective, declared in the 
configuration and reported in every log line, and still be unable to change which individuals survive; Table 7 is the 
cheapest test of that, and it costs one generation. Second, a metric's dynamic range is a necessary but not a 
sufficient repair: raising the range from 0.024 to 0.225 made the score informative without making the objective 
consequential. Third, the choice of scalarisation is itself an experimental variable. The weighted sum was inherited 
from the aggregator rather than chosen, and Section 7.6 shows that a Pareto rule would retain individuals that no 
weight at the reported scale can retain. What the framework contributes to manufacturing in this release is 
therefore the export and verification chain of Section 4 — a design is carried to STEP, DXF, bill of materials and 
an interference report, and the defects that search ignores are reported there — together with a measurement 
apparatus for the objective that the search layer consumes. Making the objective term consequential remains open; 
what is no longer open is how to find out whether it is. 
8 Limitations of the current release 
Every item below is reproducible from the repository, and each is mirrored in the repository documentation so that 
the software description and the paper cannot drift apart. 
• 
The reference body is a prototype, not an engineered design. It contains 120 colliding part pairs and eleven 
joints with implausible parameter ranges (six collapsed to [0, 0], five spanning more than 4π rad). Self-
collision penalties and physically meaningful joint limits are not yet part of the fitness function. 
• 
Control training is per-individual. A separate trainer is constructed for each evaluated individual and 
updated within that individual's budget; no single policy is shared across bodies and no zero-shot transfer is 
claimed. 
• 
The repaired objective is under-powered at the reported budget. The bound in Section 7.5 can be cleared 
by raising the weight, but the population-level benefit of doing so is not detectable with 16 individuals, 20 
generations and thirteen seeds per arm. The power calculation, now using the standard deviation measured on 
this full sample, asks for 6 seeds at a difference of 0.10 and 57 at 0.03, so the experiment is sized for the larger 
effect; the measured mean difference is indistinguishable from zero. Claims about how manufacturability 
objectives reshape evolution must therefore wait for a cheaper evaluation path, not for a better metric. 
• 
Population resolution is limited by ties. Final populations contain about nine distinct fitness values, and 31–
38% of individuals score exactly 0.0. Any added objective term competes for differences that the evaluator 
does not currently resolve, so the at-risk fraction of Section 7.5 is a bound on what a weight can achieve only 
insofar as the gaps it measures are real rather than quantisation artefacts. 
• 
The scalarisation was inherited, not chosen. Manufacturability enters as a weighted component of a scalar 
fitness because that is what the aggregator and the NSGA front construction consume. Section 7.6 shows that 
a Pareto treatment would retain individuals no weight at the reported scale can retain, so the negative result 
reported here is confined to the scalarised formulation until the three-objective run described there is carried 
out. 
• 
Geometric decomposition is not manufacturing decomposition. The parametric layer splits a body into 
parts that are individually printable and mutually placeable; it does not derive a decomposition from fixturing, 

tool access, assembly order or tolerance stack-up. A body that passes every check may therefore still require a 
decomposition a fabricator would not choose. 
• 
Manufacturability and cost aggregates coexist without reconciliation. The two manufacturability 
aggregates and the three mass and two cost estimates of Table 2 come from independent code paths that 
nothing reconciles. The two manufacturability aggregates are distinct definitions rather than an arithmetic 
error (Section 7); the mass and cost discrepancies remain unexplained. 
• 
STEP output is tessellated. Exports conform to AP242 but carry triangle meshes rather than analytic B-rep 
geometry (Section 4). 
• 
Degenerate joint ranges are reported, not rejected. Range ordering is now normalised in the genetic 
operators and the joint optimiser, so the reference snapshot contains no inverted range; but nothing constrains 
how wide a range may become, and the exporter surfaces the resulting warnings rather than refusing to export, 
so a physically implausible limit reaches the delivered files unless the reader inspects the report. 
• 
The finite-element solver discretises the bounding box, not the part. surface_to_tetrahedralize in 
forgecraft/analysis/fea.py derives its grid spacing from the part's bounding box and fills that box with 
regular volume elements; it does not tetrahedralise the part's surface geometry. Results describe a solid block 
of the part's outer dimensions and must not be read as a stress or safety-factor check. 
• 
The topology optimiser does not solve a mechanical problem. The solid isotropic material with 
penalisation (SIMP) loop in forgecraft/analysis/topology.py never assembles or solves Ku = f; spsolve 
is imported but never called, and its sensitivity term depends only on the density field. The compliance field 
of TopologyResult is hard-coded to 0.0. 
• 
Rendering is not physically based. Figures are rasterised through matplotlib with per-face Lambert/Blinn-
Phong/Fresnel shading under a fixed three-light rig — adequate for schematic views, not a path tracer, and not 
intended for material comparisons. 
• 
No performance benchmarks are shipped, and distributed evaluation is not exercised in the reported 
results. The repository contains no frames-per-second or speed-up measurements; throughput figures from 
early documentation drafts were removed rather than replaced with estimates. The Ray and multiprocessing 
paths dispatch to the same rollout-and-PPO worker used in-process and therefore agree with the single-
machine path by construction, but the experiments above use the in-process evaluator. 
• 
No evolved design has been fabricated. The manufacturing deliverables are generated and internally 
checked, but no design from this framework has been printed, assembled and tested. This is the largest gap 
between what the framework produces and what it demonstrates, and it is the next milestone rather than a 
completed result. 
9 Discussion 
Manufacturability as an experimental variable. The measurement in Section 7 reframes this question rather 
than closing it. Buildability is reported but not effectively optimised, and the reason is now specific to the point of 
being quantitative: a term added to a scalar objective can only reorder individuals whose fitness gaps are smaller 
than its own contribution, so the fraction of such pairs bounds everything any weight can achieve. That fraction 
had a median of 12–29% for one catalogue across a fortyfold weight sweep and 16–68% for the other, and the 
difference — not the weight — is what separates the two experiments. The remaining choices are engineering 
decisions rather than mysteries: compute the at-risk fraction, decide whether the reachable reordering is worth the 
run, and if not, change the formulation rather than the metric. Once that is done the interesting question becomes 
available: how does the structure of evolutionary search change when designs must be buildable? Treating part 
standardisation, minimum feature size and assembly complexity as objectives rather than filters would then turn a 
post-hoc constraint into an experimental factor. The framework makes this study possible; we have not yet carried 
it out, and Section 7 is an account of why the naive version of it does not work. 
Reproducible comparison and a bridge between two communities. By providing a tested, documented and 
permissively licensed implementation with declarative design spaces, ForgeCraft allows design spaces and tasks to 
be shared as configuration files, making comparisons between methods concrete rather than approximate. Its 
output formats target the tools mechanical engineers already use — STEP for CAD interchange, DXF for drawing 
review, bills of materials for procurement — so an evolved design can enter a mechanical workflow without being 
re-modelled from scratch, subject to the tessellation caveat above. The full pipeline also runs on commodity 
laptops with no proprietary software, which supports teaching co-design as an end-to-end engineering activity — 
from fitness function to drawing sheet — rather than as an isolated optimisation exercise. 

10 Conclusion 
ForgeCraft couples morphology–control co-evolution with a manufacturing-aware export chain, so that evolved 
robot designs are delivered together with the geometry, drawings, tolerances and documentation needed to build 
them. It integrates established components — graph-based morphology representation, GNN-encoded PPO control, 
quality-diversity and multi-objective search, MuJoCo physics — and adds an explicit representation of evolved 
bodies as parametric mechanical parts, together with an export and verification chain that reports the defects the 
search objective does not see. The paper's contribution is a measurement rather than an algorithm. Measured on 
two builds of the release, the manufacturing term never acted in the shipped one because the scoring function had 
no gradient and its weight was never read from the task file; after repair the score discriminates and the term does 
reorder comparisons, but only in a minority of them, because a scalarised term can only reorder pairs whose fitness 
gaps are smaller than its own contribution. That condition is an exact bound, computable from a single generation, 
and it explains why the same nominal weight behaves differently in two catalogues. Whether clearing the bound 
improves evolved populations remains open: at thirteen seeds per arm the measured difference is indistinguishable 
from zero, while the same power analysis asks for 6 seeds at a difference of 0.10 and 57 at 0.03. ForgeCraft is 
distributed under the MIT licence with 424 automated tests and continuous integration, and is intended as reusable 
infrastructure for research at the boundary between evolutionary robotics and design for manufacturing. 
Future work is ordered by dependency. First, the search objective needs the terms it currently lacks — self-
collision penalties and physically meaningful joint limits — and the mass and cost models must be unified into a 
single reconciled source. Second, the empirical questions the framework exists to answer come into reach: how 
manufacturability constraints reshape evolved morphologies, and how search behaves when buildability is an 
objective rather than a filter. Third, and decisively, the loop must be closed in hardware: fabricating and testing an 
evolved design is the only evidence that will establish whether the manufacturing stage reported here is a real 
capability or an elaborate serialisation format. We state the scope of this release plainly: it is a research prototype 
whose export chain is functional and exercised end to end, and whose search chain is now free of the defect that 
silenced its locomotion signal but still optimises an objective that permits self-intersecting bodies. The reference 
example is a pipeline-completeness demonstration, not a motile design.
